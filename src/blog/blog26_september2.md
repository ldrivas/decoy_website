<h3>Recent things!</h3>

*Intro*



Most of the year has disappeared into finishing my degree, working my final teaching placement at a pretty fancy private school, which in turn eventuated in handing in my resignation at a job that's occupied a pretty large part of my life for the last couple years. And now have a lot more time on my hands - enough time to actually finish a blog post. 



So, it's great having enough spare brain to have thoughts that aren't immediately distracted by a deadline I have to get to. Yay! Here's some thoughts I've had lately, brought to you from the lovely sunny alfresco seating of The Exeter.

*What I learnt about teaching*



The great thing about teaching is that it taught me that there is something that I can do forever, for hours and hours and not get sick of it. I love planning. I love getting to know students. There might not be anything I love more. When I was a child, I would often imagine how I would teach my future children the concept I was learning at the time - such as the alphabet, or basic addition. Even at five years old it was something I already understood that teaching and learning was something I had a deeprooted respect and admiration for.



I know now that I can come home after a 7-8 hour day and continue planning and not feel defeated, but often feel rejuvinated or like I'm working on something exciting. Even just planning out relatively mundane activities like a run-of-the-mill practical science experiment, or ways to engage them about learning the countries of Asia, it just feels really exciting to figure out how to share an enjoyment of learning and hopefully spark a curiosity about the wider world around us. Because I know that learning, especially in our current system, can sometimes feel a bit pointless when you don't know why you're doing it - but once you actually understand the gravity of something or become truly interested in something, it feels like magic and something you want to share with others. I really want to harbour that feeling in as many students as I can.

And it's also done wonders for my ability to self-manage complex systems and projects. I already enjoyed event planning, with tradition being putting on a Fringe show every year for the last few years (albeit sometimes in a slapdash fashion), but this feels like that on steroids. There are so many things that are required of a teacher. It's pretty mindboggling. Just to name a few things: you need to coordinate supplies/resources between classes and figure out what you need and when (and God forbid the class doesn't immediately break into chaos when you realise you don't have what you need and are forced to come up with a temporary distraction activity while you send some kid to go get it). You need to be an actor - like putting on a poker face and not laughing at a funny joke that some wisecracking kid made because it'll derail the lesson, and you need to have quick comebacks in order to earn the kids' respect, as well as understand the needs of the students in your class and predict the issues that will arise before they happen. I've really enjoyed the challenge.



On the flipside, it seems like teachers either are the best people you've ever met in life, or people who neglected to become nurses but still wanted to entertain their streak for control/bossiness. Pardon the directness in me saying that, just I met some pretty shocking teachers alongside the good ones - teachers who did nothing but seem to enjoy making their students feel like crap. I'm choosing to think of these interactions as, sometimes you have to meet flawed people to understand how to recognise and mitigate those flaws in yourself. 



Through all of this though, my experience the last couple months has helped me understand other professions too and what they entail. And their sacrifice and how nothing is all glamour. I watched Tony recently as well as probably four other chef-related movies (my boyfriend loves them and says he'd be a chef in another life) and all the stuff about the chefs working these ridiculous hours for fairly little monetary remuneration finally clicked, where it's like, huh, I'm sort of doing that too.




*What I Actually Do*



This leads me to my next point. I wanted to write a bit about what I actually do and how it makes me feel, because this is a podcast idea I have. It's where I talk to people I know and they tell me what they actually do in their jobs. Not only do I have so many friends who I don't completely understand their jobs, I also know a fair few quirky, interesting people around our town - musicians, artists, scientists, small business owners. I'd love to interview them because I'm sure they'd like others to understand what they do and why they do what they do. And then from there expand into the little things that can make a life feel meaningful. I feel a little autistic for this, because it almost feels like it's indulging my tendency to sometimes prefer talking to people in a somewhat clinical or intense fashion (there's nothing less organic than a structured interview), where I ask question after question, rather than edge gracefully into it. But I think as long as participants are willing and enthusiastic, it could be a lot of fun. Send me a message if you'd like to be involved in this if I get it up and running.



*Social Media and Pitch Perfect Performance*



Using social media makes me feel a bit yuck, but I legitimately think that unfortunately, it's actually a good way to get to know more people and make friends. And in Adelaide especially, sometimes it can be pretty hard to break out of the same one or two social scenes you inhabit without doggedly going out of your way to do so. That meme "Where do we know each other? From social media" actually feels pretty apt. It's a good way to demonstrate to people that you're like, a legibe and coherent person who knows the socially appropriate do's and don't's and has some kind of distinct taste: in this case, by demonstrating you know what is acceptable to post and not, and breaking the rules every now and then with something a little more out-there - but not excessively so. And then that in turn lowers the friction of turning acquaintances into friends. I guess it's like a grammatical language, how often to post, what degree of polish looks effortless rather than tryhard. It feels kind of like fashion in a way where it is enmeshed with social pressures and conformity and demonstrating social aptitude but there is an undeniable artistry within it.



But that doesn't stop it from feeling a little hollow. I did the whole dance with it before the weekend. I'd finished my placement, and realised that apart from the occasional birthday or a picnic and my weekly anime club, I hadn't properly socialised with much of anyone apart from my 3 or 4 close friends and my boyfriend for the last 8 weeks. So I did what any self-respecting zoomer would do - I posted a photo dump, and then a casual instagram story (with the perfectly casual caption of 1 or 2 lines - showing I'm a casual, carefree gal!). It's just funny to me how transactional it can be sometimes - proving that you have friends, interests, sense of humour, a social life, and that you are capable of understanding the norms of a particular social world, then use those newfound credits in social standing to leverage more social occasions with others. This all sounds a bit cynical, but it doesn't feel entirely dire to me, just something I wanted to actually write about rather than pretend it isn't a thing.

To wrap all this stuff about social media and real life socialising up, here's a funny and maybe hilariously bleakly on-topic thought - when I was at the Hotel Metro the other day, I had this stereotypically brainrotted apparition appear in my mind. A bar is basically an algorithmic feed. Specifically the people and conversations you experience. With enough work, you have carefully tailored it so that it will usually give you good results - great results, sometimes, even. These are the times that you happen across an expectedly stimulating or insightful conversation with an acquaintance. Maybe they tell you a secret, or you both have some realisation about something at the same time, facilitated by your serendipitous meeting. But sometimes it can give you slop, too. Real life slop would be like a weird guy that's twice your age who's playfully teasing you trying to flirt. It's kind of a luck of the draw.

*Australians and workplace culture*



I've had a thought going around my head about how Australians in corporate environments seem to drop random lore. So many coworkers tend to act mysterious and drop something massive, then give off the vibe that it didn't happen or that it wasn't a big deal.



Coworkers will be working with you normally in the open-plan, then while chatting about something normal, like plans for next year or upcoming family events, they'll drop something out of nowhere, like their father ran out on them when they were thirteen. Or they lost their license in the past due to polydrug addiction, like whatever it is. (Btw don't worry, these are not real examples)



And then, they'll just quickly move on like nothing happened. If you react with surprise, they'll respond like, "Nah nah it's fine that was ages ago." They'll say it in the most bored nonchalant tone ever. Does this happen to anyone else? It's a very strange emotional whiplash. Because you can know all these pieces about family drama or an awful childhood, so it gives you fragments of their life, but not really anything like how they felt about any of it or how they handled it. It's almost always stuff from the past so it gives the illusion of knowing someone, but not really, because you don't see them in the heat of it.



I think sometimes it stems from our inability as Australians to process emotional things without pretending they're not a big deal. And we don't really like people who appear to have a sook and whinge either. So it manifests in random trauma dumps on the people who you don't actually care about how they react, aka, your coworkers.

*Why I want to go overseas*



As I progress further into feeling more like a real adult, I feel a little less naively idealistic in some senses. But one thing that I'm not lacking in is that young kind of ambition when you're early in your career and want to make a change.



My experience in schools has kind of awoken me to so many of the issues going on in our education system, and how poorly we are dealing with it in Australia. The way we approach our curriculum in general is quite broken. This is especially true in the middle years, where the content demands are higher but students still don't quite have the world-knowledge to understand the relevance of most of the things we're subjecting them to. For an example, we love to make students make posters (because it's better than test-after-test), but all we seem to really be teaching them is how to search for and identify the correct factoid we asked them to look up, and regurgitate that fact without actually showing any understanding of context, relevance or application. This isn't really an improvement on explicit instruction (which we demonised in the early 2000s) and in some ways is worse.



I got a taste of alternative education in South Australia through working as a facilitator in project-based learning, and I hold the experience fondly, but the industry here is still in its infancy. On the bright side, it was full of heaps of fun moments and creativity. One student at the school at managed to get the admin to fund them creating a custom fursuit - so the experience was certainly full of entertainment and laughs.



In short, I'd like to go overseas and try and work in some more innovative contexts. I'm currently aiming to get some work somehow in NYC or Western Europe - I'll let you know how that goes and whether I'm successful.
