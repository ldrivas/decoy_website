This blog is incredibly overdue! I started writing this in early December last year, but only just finished it now in September!

I have been writing these travel logs as a challenge to see if I could complete a retrospective written journal as to what I did. I figured it's a bit more realistic than a stereotypical “10 Things to Do in LA” article. If you don't enjoy granular, rambly detail of holiday trips you haven't been on, then maybe give this a miss, otherwise read on.

--

I've been in America for the last two-and-a-bit weeks and am leaving in a couple more. I'm going to rapid-fire some observations about the first few cities I visited, because I otherwise don't know where to start. This covers 3 out of 7 cities on the trip (plus a 20 minute Denver cameo).

<h3>Planning & Departing</h3>
<h4><i>Why the US?</i></h4>

This is a question I got over and over leading up to the trip. If I were feeling cowardly, then I'd answer, “Well ack-tually, I have a friend who visits the US pretty frequently, and they recommended we stay together in NYC and split the accommodation.” That's part of it, but honestly, it's hard not to feel a fascination towards the country. They're like us, but a little bit different, with a rich cultural lineage. Founding fathers. Scooby-Doo. Etc.

I feel like travel experiences that are in places that are "almost" in theory like your country culturally but with key differences can sometimes give you as many rich reflections as going to a massively-different country. To me, I was very interested in what makes them culturally such a powerhouse, as well as seeing some classic sights. This decision didn't feel in spite of the political landscape, if anything it felt because of it in some ways (even if that meant I was worrying my mother a bit as to the safety aspect).

<h4><i>Planning</i></h4>

I put in some light planning for locations, dates and activities, but really didn't want to overdo it and be in a scenario where I would be enjoying a city and then had to leave because flights, accommodation and concert tickets were already booked at the next place. So I booked a couple of flights and accom that were likely to hike up majorly in price (like the ones around Thanksgiving) as well as some music or comedy shows I wanted to see like Danny Brown, but otherwise I just put together a vague plan of cities and dates.

And I did end up changing these around during the trip! Luckily hostels don't tend to creep up in price very much if at all, just flights. So this method works, as long as you don't mind bleeding at least a couple of extra hundred dollars total in last-minute flight bookings.

Admin was actually pretty straightforward:

- I didn't even need a visa since I was staying under three months, just something called an ESTA, which cost $40 and was approved immediately when I applied online
- I went with Freely travel insurance, which covered up to $100,000 for a single hospital visit (because it's the US and anything can happen with hospital debt)
- I set up Wise for banking because they seemed to have low fees and a pretty simple setup

<h4><i>Venice Beach</i></h4>

I was running out of time in LA so went to visit the boardwalk at Venice Beach and the canals. The reason this place is called Venice is because some businessman a hundred years ago wanted to take this then-deserted space and turn it into an entirely man-made American version of the Venice canals (hence the name). The business idea didn't really pan-out, but the name and the canals remain, in the form of these dingy smelly narrow spots.

<img src="/img/america/18.jpg" style="width: 50%;">


Unfortunately, the hippy vibe that you'd hear about from a Modern Lovers song is almost entirely gone - it's now a pretty commercial district. Lots of funk and soul music playing but wasn't really the venice beach that you hear about from the 70s and 80s.

<h4><i>Rapid-Fire LA Summary</i></h4>

- Pretty grimy gross, very touristy, so I was not itching to hang around
- Beverly Hills has banned traffic lights due to aesthetic reasons (this is real)
- Places I would go in California if I had more time: Joshua Tree (but not really a desert person and was likely going to Nevada later anyway), San Francisco, San Diego, Slab City
- The layout feels quite similar to Adelaide in ways. Grid layout business-district, flat, hills above, beach to the west.
- This is not the LA you'd have visualised purely based on Hollywood nostalgia or 1950s noirs, but if you know that going in then it's fine
- Fun tackiness and a worthwhile time with tempered expectations about the level of tourism rampant here
- The locals I talked to in bars & Ubers said it's hard to make friends here - people are hustling a lot, or focused on image rather than real connection

The Hollywood of now feels less like a neighbourhood or normal city hub and more like a theme-park version of itself.

As far as my meager Hollywood knolwedge goes, from the 1930s through the '50s, actors lived nearby and bars were full of working actors and directors - but obviously this changed once the city got bigger and its reputation was solidified. Even though it's now a prime example of suburban sprawling hell, I'd say it still has a fun, tacky, desperate charm that comes with people trying to make their dreams come true.

Gave myself 2 days here and that felt just about enough. Off to Austin.

<h3>Austin, Texas</h3>

<h4><i>Days 3-6:</i></h4>

<h4><i>Comedy</i></h4>

I saw a fuckton of comedy while in austin.

American audiences seem to be more lively at comedy shows, more willing to be rowdy and whistle, clap, hoot at punchlines. I guess it's the lack of tall poppy syndrome they have here. The rowdiness does mean that you get a little more heckling than in an Adelaide show, but contrary to popular belief I think a too-rowdy crowd is always better than a sea of blank faces. And a good comedian knows how to shut down a heckler anyway.

They're crazy over BBQ for some reason. A comedian mentioning barbecue in his set literally got standing applause.

<h3><i>General Observations</i></h3>

<h4><i>People</i></h4>

Like a lot of people say, Americans seem to be much more forward and self-confident in a way that Australians are not. I love how much Americans vibe loudly. It kind of feels like a video game where all the NPCs are interacting. So far, bus drivers and passengers seem much more open in their body language and incidental chatting.

Yes, it could be thought of as a superficial, surface-level warmness and openness that doesn't often go further. But it is apparent that Americans value individuality a lot more. They're more likely to just not care and be themselves and have that personal flair - even things like saying "What's up?" instead of a more typical "How are you?" when you're ordering coffee. Unironically, I heard way more fun phrases being thrown around, like "Hey man, don't worry 'bout it, I gotta roll" and that's how so many people in America actually talk. If we talked like that in Australia people think it's a bit, I guess?

<h4><i>Planning</i></h4>

I had to do so much research in every city if I wanted to actually find a good underground scene.

It was basically two-plus hours per city, using some combination of Google, Instagram, random internet forums, whatever DIY gig-tracking website people there used - which was itself often something you'd only find if you were already in the know - Viator and Facebook.

The funny thing about visiting all these cities so far is that I thought the underground scene would be easier to find because of the larger populations, but it's actually been the opposite.

The only place where this was true - where I was literally stumbling into DIY spaces quite easily - was NYC. Which, I'll talk about in Part 2.

<h4><i>Other Things</i></h4>

1. Weed smell has been pervasive in most cities and towns. Not constant, but definitely a two/three times a day kind of thing.

2. Backpackers have been fun and friendly, and generally looking to connect and have a good time.

3. It is not guaranteed that cars will give way to pedestrians.

I've gotta say that having mildly autistic traits and being into nerdy internet bullshit is kind of a superpower sometimes, because as soon as you meet someone else like that, it's almost instant trust and understanding.
