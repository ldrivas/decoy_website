<h3>You Can See Everything - Thoughts</h3>

*Intro*

To encapsulate my obvious impending love for this movie, a friend said, “This is the most Luciene-core movie ever” while I was explaining it to them, and it’s true. It’s a movie about Elizabeth Holmes and Nathan Fielder, and it left me thinking about how we lie, whether we know we’re lying and whether it matters if we know. Amongst a million gazillion other things, like the ethics of portraying someone everybody already thinks they understand. Or whether you can find something real and true in a friendship with someone who’s done something awful. It’s a very existential film. And I personally came out of it feeling strangely uplifted.

At some point, you realise that what you’re watching is not really a documentary, and the reenactments are only partial to this. It's insane how much the film does with real people, real interviews and all the ordinary materials of a documentary, but still coming off like it becomes somewhat of a fiction or a myth by the end of it.

I took copious notes right after watching the movie and have tried to reorganise them into them into subheadings. If any don’t take your interest then just skip to the next one.

*Lying*

In my (completely unprofessional) opinion, the best liars believe the lies they tell. When Nathan asks Elizabeth if she’s “ever lied in her life” I wonder how much she actually believes the way she answers this. Like, maybe she’s really convinced herself she doesn’t really lie. Maybe she’s consciously lying about lying itself. Or maybe something can be wilful at one level and feel sincere at another level.

Maybe, you can know exactly what you’re saying, and simulataneously experience a retelling as true, and still at the same time know on a level underneath that you’ve left something out. Maybe sometimes, we take a partial truth and treat it as the whole truth. So, I’d wager all of us do that at some level, it’s just a matter of how severely, how completely, and the more completely and utterly you do this, then the closer it gets to delusion.

(Alister called this reading “very Schopenhauerian”, which from my meager understanding, is around how our desires can interact with our thinking, in a way where we *think* we’re coming to our own conclusions quite independently and consciously instead of driven by compromised forces.)

Here’s an everyday example: I *know* I need to do some lesson planning before Monday. I have something in my calendar on Sunday afternoon, and I know I’ll also be preoccupied in the morning. So, I have to make time around midday, or else do the planning at night when I’m tired and crabby. Sunday rolls around and I decide I deserve to chill out before my engagement. Then, *shocked pikachu*, I have to do the lesson planning at night. I knew it was there the whole time, but somehow it still feels like a betrayal. It’s almost like I managed to delude myself that I wouldn’t have to do it.

This is a much more grim example, but I thought of this concept too when I read Craig Silvey’s 9,000 word apology that he published. Craig Silvey is a recently disgraced children’s author who I read in university as part of my children’s literature course, and he was recently found to be guilty of possessing and distributing CSM. He admitted to the offences on face value and wrote at length about his past trauma and how he’d wanted to escape his distress. And at sentencing, his lawyer made the argument that he sought out the material just to numb himself and had no sexual interest in children (this is also what Silvey echoed in his apology essay). Just the degree of detail of the chats obtained by police that were read out in court make that explanation very hard for me to take as a whole story and it feels very strange as an explanation. But the weird thing is, that Silvey might himself believe it even though it clearly does not appear to be true. And the judge also rejected this as a complete explanation of his behaviour.

So, a self-sabotage explanation has a certain rhetorical advantage. How would you prove that you’re not trying to self-sabotage by doing something horrible? You can’t. And Silvey could sincerely believe that was part of it. People defending indefensible behaviour can thrive in that ambiguity - maybe unconsciously and desperately rather than in an upfront conscious fashion. I find the subject fraught, because if some version of this is true of people on a major level, the capacity must exist in the rest of us to some degree. The lesson planning example is trivial, but I’m sure you can extrapolate it to any part of your life.

*Gender and men*

Liz definitely has elements of dissociatiation and destabilisation of identity. The Elizabeth that she was was just so jarringly different, so confident and cruel according to so many employees. This Liz seems helpless and gentle, weak and confused. It's a total 180. I'm not saying that it's not genuine, but it points to a kind of drastic switching of identities based on her environment. 

Elizabeth attaches herself to men to see the world through their eyes, through their truth. I think it's very possible that she deluded herself and made herself manipulate facts about Theranos because she wanted so much to believe in the future that Sunny wanted for Theranos.

I'm not saying that to downplay her role at all, as it still means she's guilty and committed these crimes and was abusive to her employees and everyone involved in Theranos, just it's an interesting theory for a motive. She might have been able to think of herself as a good person doing good work long after she knew the facts she was giving people were false. I find that more interesting than the idea of an evil genius consciously juggling every move.

I would believe this theory of seeking male control too, because she actually ends up attaching herself to Nathan during the documentary. Revealing far more than she needs to about her personal life, the company and her family - often to Billy's chagrin or subtle attempts to remove her from the cameras. Fielder said in an interview after the documentary came out, she had called him from prison, and again, stated she "trusted him". I don't think this is a conscious attempt to manipulate - I think just squarely that she decided Fielder was a male figure who she wanted to trust and follow as the controlled woman. And it makes sense if the theory about her seeking men who control is true - fielder certainly, like it or not, has so much control in this situation and she might be attracted (not necessarily romantically) to the idea of being under his control. I don't think this is necessarily a weird sex thing, and for all we know Billy and Elizabeth might have had 0 sexual chemistry. I think some people can be attracted to submission in ways that aren't always sexual.

*The Map and the Territory*

It felt Houellebecqian to me. Humour me briefly, for the slight tangent already - there’s a book called The Map and the Territory by the French author Michel Houellebecq, and it’s all about art and representation. The story is about an artist who starts making art out of Michelin maps, comparing them to real-life satellite photos - the artist shows how the maps pick out things like roads, lakes, places of interest, etc., and how they distort perspective and distance. He becomes a famous artist and develops notoriety as a figure himself.

It’s named after the phrase “the map is not the territory” (and in this phrase, the territory is the world itself). Basically saying, a representation will always occupy its own space and time and bias and interests. As part of this plot, Michel Houellebecq (who is a controversial and celebrity figure in France outside of his books) also put himself into it as a character, so you’re dealing with a) the author who wrote the book, b) the existing public persona outside of the book and c) the character he made out of synthesising all of those things. Obviously, this bears lot of similarities to the themes in YCSE.

Another example in the book is where the artist character tries to paint a politician, and he has so many pictures of this politician but they all give variations of the same persona (one that’s polished, and somewhat salesman-y). So, he finds that the existence of these portrayals almost self-replicate more potentially biased evidence, and his own paintings would become a portrayal of a portrayal. Since he can’t work out how to accurately paint the contradictions, he destroys the canvas rather than surrender to <a href="https://renlr.neocities.org/blog/blog25_september/">hyperreality</a>.

The point is, that representation becomes more influential than the person/thing it is portraying, and if you already know Elizabeth Holmes through The Dropout or the existing documentaries, then you’re bringing those versions into the movie with you too, and those earlier portrayals in turn become part of this movie’s portrayal. The movie explores this idea really well in a way I don't think I've ever seen in cinema.

*Cinematography*

I thought the shots were beautiful, especially the close-ups. You can see every tiny change in someone’s face while losing sight of what’s going on around them. There’s something pretty plainly ironic on a straightforward level calling it You Can See Everything when even the framing of the shots are so claustrophobic. (As an aside, my friends went to see it on Sunday and said the airconditioning was broken - they said this added to the claustrophobia, apparently.)

*Performing/Authenticity*

This entire movie really goes into Nathan Fielder’s obsession with the line between performing and being authentic and how everyone is doing some of both. For instance when we’re being sincere, we still have to select our words and regulate our expressions and think about who’s listening, just as a part of natural communication. I really feel this as an autistic person, and sometimes being authentic as a person comes with pretty immediate consequences. Like I can say the wrong thing, or come across as cold or distant or odd or whatever, or miss a social cue I might’ve needed somebody to explain to me. So, it results in this contradiction, where I might be choosing my words very carefully, and still mean them all at the same time (or at least, believe I do). I wonder if a completely unperformed self is even something another person can have access to - but I honestly wouldn’t be surprised if we couldn’t ever reach that. (I go into this in my [Her review](https://letterboxd.com/ignoranimus/film/her/) as well.)

Basically, this movie puts forward a great theory that telling the truth and being authentic and being yourself isn’t really a straightforward neat binary process.
This all also ties in also to my headcanon (and probably everyone’s at this point) that Nathan Fielder is autistic himself and has potentially navigated life having to consciously learn all these micro-expressions and social norms rather than be able to just pick them up organically and authentically. I really do think that this is where his fascination with this topic probably comes from. It feels personal (or maybe he just *wants* you to think it’s personal - 5D chess maybe?).

*Weaknesses*

Billy is one of the funniest parts of the film, and his portrayal sometimes feels a bit cheap. We spend so much time being asked to think about and consider Liz, and then the film makes it so easy to hate Billy. I don’t think it gives him the same room to be a serious character. Agree or not with the assumption he even has the potential to be a serious character, but regardless that messes with the film’s own logic a bit.

I also think, even if the film is self-referential or self-aware about what it is leaving out, it still doesn’t escape that there are potentially ethical questions about leaving out so much of the Theranos story (but I might just say this as someone who listened to the podcast and ravished any documentaries about it when I intially heard about the scandal). I could easily see people walking out of the theatre wondering if Elizabeth Holmes was not aware at all of the extent of the lying and fraud going down at Theranos - when in reality she was aware of this, and also even hired people to stalk and harass the employees who spoke out about it. And apparently, people are doing exactly that.

*Conclusion*

One reaction I keep seeing online is basically “Oh, you felt bad for her? Then damn she got you”. I felt bad for her at times and deceived by her at others and neutral at other times. And this is similar for Nathan where he seems to care about her and distrust her at the same time. She can be a loving mother and also someone who lied and still struggles to own up to what she did. It’s possible to recognise all of that and take what happened at the company seriously.

There’s an obvious question about whether you can ever really know another person. I’m more interested in what you owe them when you know enough to condemn what they did and can still only partly understand them. Also, even if someone is cut and dry immoral, can you find uniting factors and friendship in them that's real and true? I believe so. I really liked that this seemed to be a theme in the film and I feel like I haven't seen that talked about as much. The part in the movie where she said love was God, that was the realest thing I think she said in the film.

All in all, I loved that this movie lead to explorations of about 15 different themes, but most squarely, how we lie, whether we are conscious we are lying, whether it matters if we are conscious we are lying, and an interesting level of metafiction in general.
